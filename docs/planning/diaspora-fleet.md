# The Diaspora Fleet

> **Planning status — 2026-07-24:** this is the campaign-design target, not a
> complete statement of the currently playable campaign. Its vertical-slice
> requirements, pacing targets, and proposed state interactions belong to
> planning; setting-only material is kept in `docs/world/`.

## Purpose

The Diaspora Fleet is the persistent half of *Last Best Hope*. It is not an empire, a capital, or a territorial map. It is a moving civilization composed of ships, people, institutions, memories, and promises after the loss of its home.

This half gives meaning to the risks taken during Passage expeditions. It asks the player to decide what the civilization needs, what it can afford to send into danger, and what kind of future counts as survival.

The design goal is:

> Make preparation feel like commitment, and make every safe harbor require an irreversible choice.

## Player fantasy

The player is the recognized coordinator of an exodus, not an absolute ruler. They allocate scarce capacity, commission expeditions, negotiate among communities, and make emergency decisions. Their authority depends on legitimacy, trust, and the continued belief that the journey has a future.

The player should feel responsible for:

- A finite refugee population rather than an infinitely growing workforce.
- Named ships with histories, crews, loyalties, and specialized functions.
- Communities that disagree about what should be preserved.
- Cultural and biological archives that cannot all be protected.
- Settlements that become independent homes rather than owned territory.
- Promises whose consequences may arrive years later.

## Starting a chronicle

A new game begins by creating or discovering the lost civilization. The player may choose every attribute, roll everything, select a thematic origin, or lock individual results while rerolling the rest.

The sequence is:

1. Choose chronicle length.
2. Choose or roll biological and cultural attributes.
3. Define values the civilization claims to hold.
4. Generate population, communities, institutions, and ships.
5. Choose what was preserved during evacuation.
6. Establish the accepted account of the Loss and any hidden uncertainty.
7. Resolve the evacuation's material outcome.
8. Make the first pivotal decision before departure.

The first decision should be remembered throughout the chronicle. It establishes that preservation has a cost and that the player is shaping a historical people rather than selecting bonuses before a strategy match.

### Attribute classes

Civilization attributes are explicitly tagged according to their function.

**Identity attributes** generate names, descriptions, customs, event details, and cultural reactions without providing routine numeric bonuses. Examples include funerary customs, family forms, architectural motifs, ritual food, music, languages, superstition, and the cultural meaning of home.

**Capability attributes** continuously change simulation rules. Examples include biological tolerance, lifespan, reproduction, technical knowledge, automation, governance, resource efficiency, fleet condition, and archive integrity.

**Value attributes** state what the civilization believes. They become mechanical when tested by circumstances. Examples include leaving no one behind, refusing coerced settlement, preserving unaltered biology, honoring the dead, or sharing discoveries freely.

A value is not a passive bonus. It creates expectations, unlocks principled actions, and magnifies the consequences of hypocrisy. Identity attributes can also become situationally relevant: a burial tradition may matter intensely when no soil exists, even though it never grants `+5 Cohesion`.

### The founding wound

The Loss defines why the exodus exists, its urgency, what was preserved, who is blamed, and what truths may emerge later. The game generates both a public account and an underlying truth. They may agree, partially conflict, or radically diverge.

Possible Losses include catastrophe, ecological collapse, expulsion, civil war, machine revolt, failed evacuation, deliberate sacrifice, or an event nobody yet understands.

## Chronicle horizon and population scale

Every chronicle is open-ended. Seasons continue until the player concludes the
record or the traveling fleet dissolves because no active ships or population
remain. New homes, harbor networks, federations, and cultural transformation
change the ongoing history; they do not impose a timer or automatically end it.

Population scale is determined by the founding civilization rather than a
selected campaign duration. Biology, expected lifespan, fleet capacity, and the
cause of the Loss modify demographic and institutional change over time.

The simulation does not instantiate every inhabitant as a full agent. Every ship persists individually. Important captains, leaders, specialists, and historical figures are individuals. Everyone else belongs to communities, households, and age or profession cohorts. Individuals emerge from those cohorts when an event makes them historically relevant.

## Core loop

One fleet season follows this sequence:

1. **Return:** Resolve the arriving expedition's cargo, survivors, discoveries, damage, and obligations.
2. **Passage of time:** Simulate births, deaths, consumption, repairs, relationships, cultural drift, and distant settlements.
3. **Chronicle:** Summarize ordinary history and surface only needs that require strategic attention.
4. **Council:** Pause for a pivotal decision when an outcome would permanently change the civilization.
5. **Build:** Repair, refit, construct, ration, research, and reorganize the fleet.
6. **Commit:** Select an objective, ships, supplies, doctrine, and promises for the next Passage.
7. **Departure:** Remove committed ships and resources from fleet availability until the expedition returns or is declared lost.

The fleet phase should normally take five to ten minutes. It should present a few consequential decisions, not invite constant optimization of every inhabitant.

## Autonomous history and pivotal moments

Most life proceeds without player input. The simulation resolves routine production, ordinary repairs, demographic change, relationships, cultural evolution, minor accidents, and institutional activity. It periodically produces concise historical summaries.

The game pauses only when at least one outcome can permanently change:

- Where a significant population lives
- Who possesses legitimate authority
- What the civilization believes or remembers
- Which people or ships survive
- Whether a promise is honored
- Which future options remain possible
- The meaning assigned to an earlier event

Pivotal choices must alter future generation rules, not only immediate resources. Granting ships political sovereignty, for example, changes citizenship, captain autonomy, fleet fragmentation, and later settlement charters.

A standard chronicle should contain roughly 20–30 remembered civilization decisions. The ending explicitly references a smaller set of defining choices. The interface distinguishes routine operational choices from decisions likely to become historical precedents.

Important generational thresholds can generate pivotal moments. The first fleetborn majority, death of the last person who remembers the homeworld, emergence of a new language, or first settlement-born delegation should change how the civilization understands its exodus.

## The fleet as a city

Ships collectively provide the functions that a settled civilization would obtain from buildings and geography.

| Ship function | Civilization need | Consequence of loss |
|---|---|---|
| Habitats | Safe population capacity | Crowding, disease, forced settlement |
| Agricultural vessels | Reserve production | Rationing and population loss |
| Foundries | Fabrication capacity | Accumulating damage and dependence on salvage |
| Archives | Cultural continuity | Lost technologies, histories, or identities |
| Hospitals | Recovery and public health | Lasting injuries and epidemic risk |
| Survey vessels | Knowledge of routes | Blind Passage choices |
| Escorts | Protection and deterrence | Raids, coercion, and political fear |
| Colony ships | Founding new homes | Fewer opportunities to settle safely |

The fleet begins large, capable, and unsustainable. A successful campaign generally makes it smaller as people settle, ships become stations, and communities choose different futures.

## Persistent state

### Population

Population is finite and divided into communities rather than represented only by a total number. A community has:

- Population and age profile
- Skills or institutional knowledge
- Culture, language, or faith
- Political priorities
- Ships it considers its own
- Tolerance for hardship
- Position on settlement and continued travel

The player moves communities only with consent, emergency authority, or coercion. Coercive action may solve an immediate logistical problem while damaging Cohesion and future legitimacy.

Population growth occurs over decades and should not erase the meaning of major losses during a campaign.

### Ships

Ships are the primary persistent characters. Each ship records:

- Name, class, age, and original purpose
- Systems and current damage
- Captain and crew culture
- Home community or institutional affiliation
- Experience and discoveries
- Relationships with other ships
- Honors, scars, fears, and contested memories
- Previous expeditions and settlements founded

Ships must remain mechanically legible. History-derived traits should connect narrative identity to play. A vessel that repeatedly rescues others may become exceptionally capable at evacuations while refusing orders to abandon distressed ships.

### Institutions

Institutions preserve organized capabilities across ships and generations. Examples include the Admiralty, Seed Archive, Navigation Guild, Civic Assembly, a religious synod, or a machine-memory collective.

Institutions provide passive rules and unlock actions, but also develop interests. They can split, relocate to a colony, lose legitimacy, or demand resources. They are the persistent layer's closest equivalent to build-defining artifacts.

### Cultural cargo

The fleet carries irreplaceable inheritances:

- Seed and genetic banks
- Scientific and engineering corpora
- Historical records
- Languages and educational traditions
- Art, literature, ritual objects, and monuments
- Ecosystem samples
- Recorded personalities or machine intelligences
- Constitutional and legal archives

Preservation consumes mass, energy, expertise, and ship capacity. Losing an archive must change future rules rather than merely reduce a score.

## Strategic state and control

The player-facing strategic state has three axes:

- **Reserves:** consumable food, medicine, Propellant, feedstock, and spare parts.
- **Capacity:** work the fleet can perform concurrently through ships, crews,
  facilities, specialists, and institutions.
- **Cohesion:** willingness to accept collective action, derived from legitimacy,
  representation, promises, relationships, and the distribution of hardship.

The player acts through four control families: **Allocation** assigns reserves and
capacity; **Policy** sets standing rules for autonomous history; **Organization**
determines who controls capabilities; and **Commitment** reserves future action and
stakes trust on delivery. This is a state-by-control matrix, not twelve currencies.

Information overlays every decision. Discoveries and records persist; archives
retain them, while ships, specialists, compute, and institutions determine whether
they can be applied. Forecasts may be known, bounded, disputed, or unknown.

Sustenance, feedstock, Propellant, knowledge records, labor, and institutional legitimacy
remain detailed simulation state. They are exposed when they explain a forecast or
decision, not promoted into parallel global wallets.

## Needs and political pressure

Each season surfaces two or three urgent needs. Examples:

- Agricultural output will fall below consumption next season.
- A community demands settlement within three years.
- An old escort needs a reactor that could instead power a colony.
- An archive requires specialists currently assigned to navigation.
- A distant settlement requests defense.
- Children born during the exodus demand representation.
- A rescued population wants its ships and authority restored.

The player cannot satisfy every need. Neglected needs should generate understandable consequences and new situations rather than silently subtracting points.

Promises are explicit persistent objects. A promise records its beneficiary, condition, deadline, and expected remedy. Breaking one changes relationships and how future commitments are received.

## Building and refitting

The Build step offers a limited project queue. Projects include:

- Repair a ship or stabilize a damaged institution
- Refit a vessel for a different role
- Construct a small ship from salvaged hulls
- Expand habitats or life support
- Analyze an expedition discovery
- Preserve a temporary alien modification
- Prepare a colony package
- Restore or copy an archive
- Produce supplies for the next Passage

Projects consume resources, time, specialists, and physical facilities. A refit should usually change how a ship combines with others rather than provide only a numeric increase.

## Founding a home

Settlement is the central irreversible action of the fleet phase. A colony package may include:

- Volunteers and dependents
- Habitat or colony vessels
- Agricultural and industrial capacity
- Specialists
- Cultural archives
- Defensive ships
- Political charter
- Initial reserves

Everything committed leaves the traveling fleet. A generous package improves the settlement's prospects while weakening the exodus. A minimal package preserves fleet capability but risks creating a failed or authoritarian colony.

Settlements are not player-owned production nodes. After founding, they become autonomous actors. The fleet may receive delayed messages, trade, requests, migrants, political criticism, or silence. Revisited settlements should reflect their environment, founding population, charter, resources, and the player's earlier choices.

## Fleet contraction as progress

The normal strategy-game arc of continuous accumulation is inverted. Campaign progress is visible through:

- Fewer refugees requiring uncertain passage
- More independent settlements behind the fleet
- Ships converted into permanent infrastructure
- Cultural traditions taking root in different forms
- A smaller but increasingly storied traveling fleet

The interface should commemorate departures. When a ship remains as a colony's first orbital station, it leaves the active roster but enters the historical record.

## Failure, survival, and victory

The persistent campaign rarely ends because of one failed expedition. Pressure accumulates through population loss, fragmentation, depleted Reserves, damaged ships, and collapsing Cohesion.

At zero Cohesion, the fleet enters a constitutional emergency: communities may split, impose new leadership, force settlement, or choose incompatible routes. The player gets a final crisis response rather than an immediate game-over.

Experienced players on standard difficulty should secure some viable future in roughly 75% of campaigns. The harder measures are qualitative:

- How many people found safety?
- Which institutions survived?
- Were settlements free, viable, and mutually supportive?
- What promises were broken?
- Did the civilization preserve its identity, transform it, or intentionally let it go?

Possible victories include one major New Home, a network of independent harbors, a sustainable nomadic fleet, federation with another civilization, return to the lost home, or deliberate biological/cultural transformation.

## Interface with Passage

The fleet sends:

- Named ships and their crews
- Supplies, Propellant, and cargo capacity
- A primary objective
- Doctrine and acceptable-risk policy
- Political promises
- Optional settlement package

The fleet receives:

- Surviving ships, damage, and new traits
- Extracted cargo and rescued people
- Route information and discovered worlds
- Temporary technology that may be made permanent
- Diplomatic obligations and enemies
- Evidence that changes public understanding
- Completed, failed, or transformed objectives

No expedition result should be reduced to a single reward number. Its return must create at least one new fleet decision.

## Story cadence

Routine history should occupy real time. Major systemic beats—fleet hazards,
constitutional disputes, settlement decisions, and comparable reversals—leave
at least one clear season between them. Player-commissioned Passage expeditions
may create immediate consequences, but the scheduler does not stack another
unrelated major beat into the same season.

The opening season surfaces three needs to establish the strategic board.
Later seasons vary their new needs according to story tempo. Existing needs
keep their deadlines and consequences; cadence delays new petitions rather
than silently removing unresolved ones.

Story tempo is configured separately from combat heroism. **Measured** leaves
one clear season between major beats. **Spacious** leaves at least two and
reduces incoming petitions. **Volatile** permits consecutive major beats and
usually surfaces two or more petitions. The selected tempo changes timing and
pressure, not the deterministic outcome of an already-scheduled event.

## Vertical-slice requirements

The first complete Fleet prototype should support:

- 12 named ships and 4 communities
- Reserves, Capacity, and Cohesion, operated through Allocation, Policy,
  Organization, and Commitment
- Three competing seasonal needs
- Repair and role-changing refits
- Expedition commissioning
- One settlement candidate and colony-package decision
- Delayed settlement outcome
- Ship history generated from expedition results
- An open-ended campaign with at least three conclusion classifications
