# Writing guidelines

Use these guidelines for setup text, event records, ship histories, Passage
contacts, choices, reports, debriefs, precedents, and endings.

## Leave interpretation to the player

State what happened, what was said, and what changed. Do not use an
authoritative voice to certify a person's feelings, a community's motives, or
an event's historical meaning.

Prefer:

> Common Hearth broke formation and took the survivors aboard.

Avoid:

> In a defining act of compassion, Common Hearth proved what the fleet had
> always stood for.

The simulation may record a precedent or a violated promise. It should not also
tell the player how to feel about it.

## Separate record from interpretation

The chronicle may state observable or committed facts:

- ships departed, returned, suffered damage, or were lost;
- resources, populations, routes, and institutions changed;
- a promise was made, upheld, broken, or reinterpreted;
- a council enacted a named precedent;
- a speaker used particular words.

Do not present inference as fact:

- a captain acted from fear, guilt, pride, or ambition;
- a community secretly wanted an outcome;
- a sacrifice was noble, necessary, or foolish;
- an event marked the moment the fleet “became” something.

A character or faction may make those judgments. Attribute them to the speaker.
An ending may assemble consequences the player has already established, but it
should not retroactively explain every choice.

## Keep copy proportional to the decision

Most UI text should be one line. Use one short sentence for a choice's basic
effect or cultural meaning. Add a second sentence only when the player needs it
to own an irreversible risk.

- Operational label: a phrase.
- Tooltip or setup description: one sentence.
- Event record: one concrete event.
- Ship history note: one memorable fact.
- Major decision: stakes, cost, and irreversible consequence.
- Debrief: facts first; interpretation only when attributed or earned.

Do not repeat the button label in prose. Do not explain a story beat after
showing it. Cut throat-clearing such as “This represents,” “This reflects,” and
“This moment will define.”

## Reveal consequences, not future story

Give the player enough information to own a choice:

- the resource or capability spent;
- the known danger range;
- ships, people, archives, or opportunities placed at risk;
- the rule an option uses or unlocks;
- whether a decision is reversible, persistent, or precedent-setting.

Do not preview surprise outcomes, future encounters, hidden truths, or the
eventual moral interpretation. Bounded uncertainty is useful; missing rules are
not. If a choice has no implemented effect, fix or remove it rather than imply
that unseen consequences exist.

## Let history accumulate

Do not announce importance before the record earns it. Repetition, later
reference, mechanical persistence, and changed behavior make an event
historical.

Prefer specific continuity:

> Resolute still carries the breach opened at Ilex Gate.

Avoid generic significance:

> Resolute bears the scars of a journey that changed everyone forever.

Reuse exact names for ships, communities, promises, precedents, and places.
When later text recalls an event, preserve its consequential wording.

## Give ships and communities distinct vocabularies

Ships are persistent characters. Build voice from repeated conduct, duties,
relationships, damage, and choices—not interchangeable aphorisms.

- A survey ship notices bearings, signals, margins, and unknowns.
- A hospital ship counts lives, capacity, exposure, and time.
- A foundry ship speaks through tolerances, repair, substitution, and labor.
- Communities refer to their own institutions, customs, losses, and promises.

Do not make every voice polished, prophetic, or equally self-aware. Ceremonial
language should be rare enough to remain ceremonial.

## Pace one consequence at a time

Present complex outcomes as an ordered sequence of short records. One entry
should contain one action, statement, or state change. Let the player advance
between consequential beats when order matters.

Do not compress departure, crisis, sacrifice, return, and historical judgment
into one paragraph. Do not split a single minor outcome into several dramatic
screens.

## Use restrained fleet language

The voice is archival, humane, and concrete. It may be solemn, but it is not
constantly elegiac. Prefer plain verbs and named costs over abstractions.

Use words such as *defining*, *sacred*, *last*, *forever*, *hope*, and *memory*
sparingly. They are strongest in names, vows, and genuinely terminal moments.
Avoid stacking metaphors or ending routine notices with an aphorism.

## Review checklist

Before player-facing text is complete, check that:

- it reports facts without certifying motive or moral meaning;
- its length matches the importance and complexity of the decision;
- rules, costs, and irreversible consequences are visible;
- surprises and hidden truths remain hidden;
- each event entry contains one legible beat;
- names and consequential wording remain consistent;
- ships and communities sound grounded in their histories and roles;
- no sentence merely restates the UI or explains the previous sentence;
- removing an explanatory clause makes the beat sharper, not confusing;
- the text still works when read as part of a long chronicle, not only alone.
